How your childhood experiences shape your adult relationships

Recognising childhood emotional wounds, and the protective behaviours and patterns we carry into our adult relationships.

22 November 2026 · By Sharon Tomlin

Have you ever wondered why you can feel deeply affected by certain situations? Maybe you worry about being rejected, struggle to trust others, feel responsible for everyone's moods or become anxious when a loved one seems distant.

Our childhood experiences play an important role in shaping how we see ourselves, how we relate to others and what we expect from our relationships. Early experiences that occurred many years ago can still influence our emotional responses, beliefs and behaviour.

This doesn't mean that our childhood determines our future. Understanding how our early experiences shaped us can be an important step towards developing healthier relationships and a kinder relationship with ourselves.

Our childhood experiences form an emotional imprint

As children, we depend on the adults around us for love, security, reassurance and emotional support. We need to feel accepted, protected, listened to and understood.

When these emotional needs are consistently met, we are more likely to develop a secure sense of self and feel comfortable forming close relationships. Unfortunately, not everyone grows up experiencing this.

Some children experience emotional neglect, criticism, rejection, inconsistency, conflict or a lack of affection. Some grow up feeling that they need to be well behaved at all times, achieve highly or look after the emotional needs of their parents.

These experiences don't always involve obvious trauma or a single event. Sometimes, it is the repeated absence of what a child needs that leaves the deepest emotional impact.

A child who doesn't receive reassurance may begin to feel they are unimportant; a child who receives frequent criticism may believe they are never good enough. These beliefs become deeply embedded and influence our adult relationships.

The coping mechanisms we develop as a child

One of the most interesting aspects of understanding childhood experiences is recognising that many of the behaviours we struggle with as adults developed as ways of protecting ourselves. For example:

  • A child who has grown up around anger or conflict may learn to avoid disagreements and become a people pleaser
  • A child who experiences rejection might become anxious about relationships and seek constant reassurance
  • A child who experiences criticism might become a perfectionist, believing they have to get everything right to be accepted
  • A child whose feelings are dismissed might learn to suppress their emotions and struggle to express their needs

During childhood, these behaviours may have helped the child to cope with difficult circumstances.

How our childhood experiences affect our nervous system

Our early experiences don't just influence our thoughts and beliefs. They can also influence how our nervous system responds to situations. When we experience something that feels threatening, our nervous system can respond with our fight, flight or freeze response to protect ourselves.

If you grew up in an unpredictable or emotionally difficult environment, your nervous system may have become particularly sensitive to signs of conflict, rejection or danger. This can mean that seemingly small situations trigger disproportionately strong emotional reactions. For example:

  • A partner becoming quiet may trigger anxiety or fear of abandonment
  • Someone raising their voice might create a feeling of threat
  • A disagreement might feel frightening even when the relationship is secure
  • Emotional distance might trigger a strong need to seek reassurance
  • Feeling criticised may bring back feelings of shame or inadequacy

We might understand that we are safe, yet emotionally and physically we can react as if something threatening is happening.

Learning to recognise when we feel overwhelmed, and developing ways to calm and regulate ourselves, can help us respond more thoughtfully rather than reacting from old patterns.

The beliefs we carry into adulthood

Our childhood experiences can also shape the beliefs we develop about ourselves and others. In psychological terms, these deeply rooted patterns are sometimes referred to as schemas. Schemas influence how we interpret situations, and how we feel and behave.

Some common beliefs that develop from difficult childhood experiences include:

  • I'm not good enough
  • I'm difficult to love
  • People will eventually leave me
  • My needs aren't important
  • I have to earn people's affection
  • I cannot trust anyone
  • I must keep everyone happy

These beliefs can influence the relationships we choose and how we behave within them. They can feel like facts rather than ideas we developed in response to our experiences.

Breaking these patterns begins with recognising what is happening and understanding what we need from relationships today, rather than continuing to seek what may have been missing in childhood.

Healing begins with understanding, not blaming

Exploring our childhood experiences isn't about blaming our parents or becoming stuck in the past. It's about recognising that we were children who adapted to the circumstances we were given, often without the emotional support or understanding we needed.

Perhaps you learnt to be the strong one, the responsible one or the one who never caused problems. Perhaps you became particularly sensitive to others' moods because you needed to anticipate how they might react.

These behaviours may have helped you manage your childhood, but you don't necessarily need to carry them into every aspect of your adult life.

An important part of healing is learning to offer yourself some of the understanding, reassurance and compassion that may have been missing earlier in life. Rather than asking ‘what's wrong with me?’, you may begin to ask:

  • What happened to me that might help explain why I feel this way?
  • What did I need as a child that I didn't receive?
  • What am I afraid might happen in relationships?
  • Which of my behaviours are protecting me from getting hurt?
  • How can I begin to respond to myself with greater kindness?

These questions can help us develop a deeper understanding of ourselves and the patterns we have carried into adulthood.

How counselling can help

Counselling offers a safe and supportive space to explore how your childhood experiences may be influencing your adult relationships. Sometimes we recognise that we are unhappy in our relationships but struggle to understand why we keep responding in certain ways.

This may involve looking at family relationships, attachment, emotional needs, boundaries, self-esteem and the coping strategies we have developed over time. It is important that we explore this at a manageable pace. Healing isn't only about revisiting painful memories; it is also about developing understanding, emotional resources and the self-compassion needed to move forward.

With greater awareness, we can begin to recognise that the ways we learnt to cope as children don't have to determine how we live as adults.

You can't change the childhood you had, but you can begin to change the relationship you have with yourself and the way you relate to others.
